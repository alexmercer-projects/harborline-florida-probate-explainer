Do I have to go through probate in Florida? Summary vs. formal administration, explained

Not every Florida estate needs a full court case. Here is how to tell which of the three paths fits, what changed on July 1, 2026, and which assets never go through probate at all.

The short answer

Maybe not. Probate only deals with things the person owned in their own name, with no co-owner or beneficiary set to take over. If there is little or nothing like that, you may need a short court process or none at all.

When it is needed, summary administration is the shorter type. It can fit when the Florida probate estate, not counting property exempt from creditors, is $150,000 or less, or the person died more than 2 years ago. Everything else goes through formal administration, where the court appoints a personal representative who generally must have a Florida lawyer.

What probate actually covers

Probate is the court process for gathering what a person owned, paying their debts and passing on the rest. It only reaches probate assets: as The Florida Bar's consumer pamphlet puts it, things owned in the person's sole name, or shared with no automatic transfer at death. For example:

  • A bank or brokerage account in their name alone, with no payable-on-death or transfer-on-death beneficiary.
  • Real estate in their name alone, or held with others as tenants in common. A homestead has its own rules, covered below.
  • A life insurance policy or retirement account that names "my estate" as the beneficiary, or names no one.

What usually passes outside probate

These assets go straight to the named person or co-owner, without a probate order:

  • Payable-on-death bank accounts (§ 655.82) and transfer-on-death brokerage accounts (§ 711.509).
  • Life insurance, annuities and retirement accounts with a living, named beneficiary.
  • Property owned with survivorship, such as a home spouses own as tenants by the entirety, or a deed that says "joint tenants with right of survivorship."
  • Assets already retitled into a revocable living trust. The trustee handles them under the trust's terms.

Watch the wording on deeds. A Florida deed to two or more people who are not married to each other creates a tenancy in common unless it expressly says survivorship (§ 689.15). Being named "jointly" may not be enough.

Outside probate is not always out of reach of creditors. The transfer-on-death law leaves creditors' other rights in place (§ 711.509(2)), and a revocable trust can be made to pay estate debts if the probate estate falls short (§ 733.707(3)).

The three paths for a Florida estate

1. No administration: very small estates

Under § 735.301, no administration is needed when the person left only exempt personal property plus other personal property worth no more than the funeral bill and the reasonable medical and hospital costs of the last 60 days of their final illness. An interested person, usually a family member, asks the court informally, often by affidavit, and the court authorizes the transfer in a letter. The 2026 law also raised the amounts for two smaller shortcuts:

  • A bank may release up to $2,000 in sole-name accounts to a spouse or close family member by affidavit, but not until 6 months after the death (§ 735.303).
  • For a person who died without a will more than a year ago, heirs can ask for personal property up to $20,000, plus funeral and final medical costs, by affidavit (§ 735.304).

2. Summary administration: the shorter court process

Summary administration is still a court case, but a short one. Under § 735.201 it is available when both of these are true:

  1. If there is a will, it does not require formal administration.
  2. The Florida estate subject to administration, minus property exempt from creditors, is $150,000 or less, or the person has been dead for more than 2 years.

New in 2026

The limit was $75,000 until chapter 2026-57, Laws of Florida, doubled it on July 1, 2026. Many websites, and The Florida Bar's 2021 pamphlet, still say $75,000. The new law does not say whether the date of death matters. If the person died before July 1, 2026 and the estate is between $75,000 and $150,000, ask a probate lawyer how your court is handling it.

The surviving spouse and the beneficiaries sign and verify a petition (§ 735.203). Any will is admitted to probate. Before the order is signed, the petitioner must search diligently for creditors, send them the petition and provide for paying them from available assets (§ 735.206). The court can then order the assets distributed right away.

No personal representative is appointed. The court's order itself tells banks who gets what. That makes it faster, but it also means no one has ongoing authority to act for the estate.

The trade-off. The people who receive assets stay personally responsible for their share of valid claims, up to the value of what they received, until claims run out 2 years after the death (§ 735.206(4)). Publishing a notice to creditors can cut off unknown creditors after 3 months (§ 735.2063).

3. Formal administration: the standard probate case

Formal administration is the default. The court appoints a personal representative, Florida's term for an executor or administrator, and issues "letters of administration" giving that person legal authority to act for the estate.

Under Florida Probate Rule 5.030(a), a personal representative must have a Florida attorney unless they are the only interested person. Someone who lives outside Florida can serve only if they are a close relative, such as a child, grandchild, parent, sibling, aunt, uncle, niece or nephew, or the spouse of one (§ 733.304).

The main steps:

  1. Notice of administration. The personal representative serves it on the spouse and beneficiaries. They generally have 3 months after service to object to the will, the venue or the personal representative (§ 733.212).
  2. Notice to creditors. It runs in a local newspaper once a week for 2 weeks in a row, and known creditors get a copy (§ 733.2121).
  3. Claims deadline. Most claims must be filed by the later of 3 months after first publication or 30 days after a creditor is served (§ 733.702). No matter what, most claims are barred 2 years after death (§ 733.710).
  4. Inventory, bills, taxes and distribution, then a final accounting and discharge.

It takes longer, but it gives a clean cutoff for creditors and someone with clear authority to sell property or handle a dispute. Some families choose it even when the estate would qualify for summary administration.

How the $150,000 is counted

The test looks only at the Florida estate "subject to administration," minus property exempt from creditors. Big items often drop out:

  • Anything that passes outside probate, from the list above.
  • Protected homestead. Florida's constitution shields most homesteads from creditors (Art. X, § 4), and a protected homestead is not an asset the personal representative controls (§ 733.608). Families usually ask the court to confirm homestead status. A rental or second home is not homestead.
  • Exempt property under § 732.402: household furniture and appliances up to $20,000 in net value, up to two personal cars or trucks, and 529 college savings plans. It applies if the person lived in Florida, and the spouse or children must claim it, generally within 4 months after the notice of administration is served.

Say a Florida resident died in August 2026, leaving a spouse, a paid-off homestead, two cars and $120,000 in savings in their own name. That estate could still qualify, because the house and the cars drop out of the count.

One more homestead deadline. If the person left a spouse and descendants and the homestead was not left by will, the spouse gets a life estate, or can choose a half share within 6 months after the death (§ 732.401).

Which path fits? A quick check

    Did the person own anything in their name alone, with no co-owner or beneficiary to take it?

    No Probate is probably not needed. Each asset is usually claimed directly with a death certificate.

    Yes Go to question 2.

  2. 2

    Is it only exempt property, plus personal property worth no more than the funeral and final medical bills?

    Yes No administration may fit (§ 735.301).

    No Go to question 3.

  3. 3

    Does the will say the estate must go through formal administration?

    Yes Formal administration.

    No, or there is no will Go to question 4.

  4. 4

    Yes Summary administration may fit. Check question 5.

    No Formal administration.

  5. 5

    Are there debts you can't cover, a property to sell or heirs who disagree?

    Yes Ask about formal administration anyway. A firm creditor deadline may be worth the extra time.

    No Summary administration is likely the simpler path.

Frequently asked questions

Can a family handle summary administration without a lawyer?

Rule 5.030's attorney requirement is written for personal representatives, and summary administration does not appoint one. Some families do file on their own. Whether that is wise depends on the will, the debts and whether everyone agrees, because the people who sign the petition can be held responsible later if a creditor or an heir was missed.

Does the house count toward the $150,000 summary administration limit?

Usually not, if it was the person's protected homestead, because the limit leaves out property that is exempt from creditors. The court still needs to confirm homestead status. A rental, vacation home or empty lot is not homestead and does count.

What if the person died more than two years ago?

Summary administration is then available no matter how large the estate is, unless the will requires formal administration. Most claims against the person are also barred after two years, with exceptions such as recorded mortgages.

Do debts still have to be paid in summary administration?

Yes. The petitioner must search for creditors, send them the petition and provide for payment from available assets before the court signs the order. People who receive assets can be personally responsible for their share of valid claims, up to what they received.

Does a living trust mean no probate at all?

Assets retitled into the trust pass outside probate. Anything left in the person's own name may still need probate, often through a pour-over will. And trust assets can be used for estate expenses and claims if the probate estate runs short.
