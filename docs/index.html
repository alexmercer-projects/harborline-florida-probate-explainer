Do I have to go through probate in Florida? Summary vs. formal administration, explained

Not every Florida estate needs a full court case. Here is how to tell which of the three paths fits, what changed on July 1, 2026, and which assets never go through probate at all.

  • Updated Nov. 22, 2026
  • About 9 minutes to read
  • Reflects the July 1, 2026 change to the summary administration limit

The short answer

Maybe not. Probate only deals with things the person owned in their own name, with no co-owner or beneficiary set to take over. If there is little or nothing like that, you may need a short court process or none at all.

When probate is needed, Florida has two main types. Summary administration is the shorter one. It can fit when the Florida probate estate, not counting property exempt from creditors, is $150,000 or less, or the person died more than 2 years ago. Everything else goes through formal administration, where the court appoints a personal representative who generally must have a Florida lawyer.

What probate actually covers

Probate is the court process for gathering what a person owned when they died, paying their debts and passing the rest to the right people. In Florida the rules sit in chapters 731 to 735 of the Florida Statutes and in the Florida Probate Rules.

The key point is that probate only reaches probate assets. As The Florida Bar's consumer pamphlet puts it, these are things owned in the person's sole name, or shared with someone else with no automatic transfer at death. Common examples:

  • A bank or brokerage account in their name alone, with no payable-on-death or transfer-on-death beneficiary.
  • Real estate in their name alone, or held with others as tenants in common. A homestead has its own rules, covered below.
  • A life insurance policy or retirement account that names "my estate" as the beneficiary, or names no one.

What usually passes outside probate

These assets go straight to the named person or co-owner, without a probate order:

  • Payable-on-death bank accounts (§ 655.82) and transfer-on-death brokerage accounts (§ 711.509).
  • Life insurance, annuities and retirement accounts with a living, named beneficiary.
  • Property owned with survivorship, such as a home spouses own as tenants by the entirety, or a deed that says "joint tenants with right of survivorship."
  • Assets already retitled into a revocable living trust. The trustee handles them under the trust's terms.

Watch the wording on deeds. In Florida, a deed to two or more people who are not married to each other creates a tenancy in common unless it expressly says there is a right of survivorship (§ 689.15). A parent and child named "jointly" on a deed may not get the result they expected.

"Outside probate" does not always mean "out of reach of creditors." Florida law keeps some creditor rights against transfer-on-death assets (§ 711.509(2)), and a revocable trust can be made to pay estate expenses and claims if the probate estate falls short (§ 733.707(3)).

The three paths for a Florida estate

Once you know what is left in the person's own name, the question becomes how much court involvement it needs. There are three main answers.

1. No administration: very small estates

Florida lets some estates skip a formal case entirely. Under § 735.301, no administration is needed when the person left only exempt personal property plus other personal property worth no more than the funeral bill and the reasonable medical and hospital costs of the last 60 days of their final illness. A family member asks the court informally, often by affidavit, and the court authorizes the transfer in a letter.

A few smaller shortcuts also exist, and the 2026 law raised several of the dollar amounts:

  • A bank may release up to $2,000 in sole-name accounts to a spouse or close family member by affidavit, but not until 6 months after the death (§ 735.303).
  • For a person who died without a will more than a year ago, heirs can ask for personal property up to $20,000, plus funeral and final medical costs, by affidavit (§ 735.304).
  • A federal tax refund of up to $5,000 can go to the surviving spouse or children if no administration is planned (§ 735.302).

2. Summary administration: the shorter court process

Summary administration is still a court case, but a short one. Under § 735.201 it is available when both of these are true:

  1. If there is a will, it does not require formal administration.
  2. The Florida estate subject to administration, minus property exempt from creditors, is $150,000 or less, or the person has been dead for more than 2 years.

New in 2026

The limit was $75,000 until July 1, 2026, when chapter 2026-57, Laws of Florida, doubled it. Many websites, and even The Florida Bar's pamphlet (last updated in 2021), still show $75,000. The new law does not say whether it depends on the date of death. If the person died before July 1, 2026 and the estate falls between $75,000 and $150,000, ask a probate lawyer how your court is treating it.

Here is how it works. The surviving spouse and the beneficiaries sign and verify a petition (§ 735.203). Any will is proved and admitted to probate. Before the order is signed, the person filing must make a diligent search for creditors, send them the petition and provide for paying them from the assets that are available (§ 735.206). The court can then order the assets distributed right away.

No personal representative is appointed. The court's order itself tells banks and others who gets what. That is what makes it faster. It also means no one has ongoing authority to act for the estate.

The trade-off. The people who receive assets stay personally responsible for their share of valid claims, up to the value of what they received, until claims run out 2 years after the death (§ 735.206(4)). Publishing a notice to creditors can cut off unknown creditors after 3 months (§ 735.2063).

3. Formal administration: the standard probate case

Formal administration is the default. The court appoints a personal representative, Florida's term for an executor or administrator, and issues "letters of administration" giving that person legal authority to act for the estate.

Under Florida Probate Rule 5.030(a), a personal representative must be represented by a Florida attorney unless they are the only person with an interest in the estate. Someone who lives outside Florida can serve only if they are a close relative, such as a child, parent, sibling, niece or nephew, or the spouse of one (§ 733.304).

The main steps:

  1. Notice of administration. The personal representative serves it on the spouse and beneficiaries. They generally have 3 months after service to object to the will, the venue or the personal representative (§ 733.212).
  2. Notice to creditors. It runs in a local newspaper once a week for 2 weeks in a row, and known creditors get a copy (§ 733.2121).
  3. Claims deadline. Most claims must be filed by the later of 3 months after first publication or 30 days after a creditor is served (§ 733.702). No matter what, most claims are barred 2 years after death (§ 733.710).
  4. Inventory, bills, taxes and distribution. Then a final accounting and discharge.

Formal administration takes longer, but it gives a clean cutoff for creditors and a person with clear authority to sell property, collect debts or handle a dispute. Some families choose it even when the estate would qualify for summary administration.

How the $150,000 is counted

The summary administration test looks only at the Florida estate "subject to administration," minus property exempt from creditors. Several big items often drop out:

  • Anything that passes outside probate, from the list above.
  • Protected homestead. Florida's constitution shields most homesteads from creditors (Art. X, § 4), and a homestead is not an asset the personal representative controls (§ 733.608). The court still has to determine homestead status. A rental or second home is not homestead.
  • Exempt property under § 732.402: household furniture and appliances up to $20,000 in net value, up to two personal cars or trucks, and 529 college savings plans. The spouse or children must claim it, within 4 months after service of the notice of administration in a formal case.

So a person could leave a paid-off homestead, two cars and $120,000 in a sole-name savings account, and the estate could still qualify. The house, if confirmed as homestead, and the cars would be left out of the count.

One more homestead deadline. If the person left a spouse and children and the homestead was not left by will, the spouse gets a life estate. The spouse can instead choose a half share, but must do so within 6 months after the death (§ 732.401).

Which path fits? A quick check

Answer these in order. It is a starting point for a conversation, not a final answer.

  1. 1

    Did the person own anything in their name alone, with no co-owner or beneficiary to take it?

    No Probate is probably not needed. Collect each asset with a death certificate and the account's own claim process.

    Yes Go to question 2.

  2. 2

    Is it only exempt property, plus personal property worth no more than the funeral and final 60 days of medical bills?

    Yes No administration may fit (§ 735.301).

    No Go to question 3.

  3. 3

    Does the will say the estate must go through formal administration?

    Yes Formal administration.

    No, or there is no will Go to question 4.

  4. 4

    Is the Florida probate estate, minus exempt property, $150,000 or less? Or did the person die more than 2 years ago?

    Yes Summary administration may fit. Check question 5.

    No Formal administration.

  5. 5

    Are there unpaid debts you can't cover, a property to sell, heirs who disagree, or a lawsuit to bring?

    Yes Ask about formal administration anyway. A personal representative and a firm creditor deadline may be worth the extra time.

    No Summary administration is likely the simpler path.

Common mistakes we see families try to avoid

  • Using the old $75,000 number. Check the date on anything you read.
  • Skipping the creditor search in summary administration. A missed creditor can come back to the people who signed the petition, and can recover attorney's fees.
  • Missing the quiet deadlines. The spouse's 6-month homestead election and the exempt property claim are easy to lose.
  • Assuming "joint" means survivorship. Read the deed or account paperwork.

Frequently asked questions

Can a family handle summary administration without a lawyer?

Florida Probate Rule 5.030 requires a personal representative to have a Florida attorney unless that person is the only interested person. Summary administration does not appoint a personal representative, so that rule is aimed at formal administration. Some families file summary petitions on their own. Whether that is wise depends on the will, the debts and whether everyone agrees, because the people who sign the petition can be held responsible later if a creditor or an heir was missed.

Does the house count toward the $150,000 summary administration limit?

Usually not, if it was the person's protected homestead. The limit is measured after leaving out property that is exempt from creditors, and Florida's constitution protects most homesteads from creditors. The court still has to confirm homestead status. A rental, vacation home or empty lot is not homestead and does count.

What if the person died more than two years ago?

Summary administration is available no matter how large the estate is, as long as the will does not require formal administration. After two years, most claims against the person are also barred by section 733.710, with exceptions such as recorded mortgages and claims already filed.

Do debts still have to be paid in summary administration?

Yes. Before the court signs the order, the petitioner must search for known or reasonably findable creditors, send them the petition and provide for payment from the assets that are available. People who receive assets can be personally responsible for their share of valid claims, up to the value of what they received.

How long does formal administration take in Florida?

It depends on the estate. The creditor period alone keeps an estate open for at least three months, and The Florida Bar's consumer pamphlet says a simple estate can reasonably be expected to take about five or six months. Selling real estate, disputed claims or a will contest can add a lot of time.

Does a living trust mean no probate at all?

Assets that were retitled into the trust pass outside probate. Anything left in the person's own name with no beneficiary may still need probate, often through a short pour-over will. The trustee also has to deal with debts, and trust assets can be used for estate expenses and claims if the probate estate runs short.
